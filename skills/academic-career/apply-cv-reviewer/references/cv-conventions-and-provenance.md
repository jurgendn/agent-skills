# Academic CV conventions — what is convention, what is contested

Distilled from nine university career-office pages, accessed 2026-09-08. None of the
nine carried a visible publication or last-reviewed date, so treat every specific below as
undated and route anything binding to the live call.

## The load-bearing distinction: which CV is being reviewed

Most published academic-CV guidance is written for the **faculty and postdoc job market**, not
for someone applying **to** a graduate programme. The two audiences get opposite advice, and
importing the job-market norms into an applicant's CV produces confidently wrong review
findings.

Of the nine sources, eight are job-market or industry oriented, or do not state their audience.
Exactly one addresses graduate applicants directly, and it disagrees with the rest:

| | Job-market sources | Applicant source (Oxford) |
|---|---|---|
| Length | MIT 2–7 pp; Harvard GSAS 2–5 pp; Berkeley 3–5 pp; Northeastern ≤6 pp early career; Yale "several pages" | "between one and two pages" |
| Completeness | MIT: "completeness is more important than brevity"; Northeastern: "often nothing is removed" | selective; "expand on something specific… in your personal statement" |
| Is a CV wanted? | assumed | "Most courses do not require a CV (résumé)… only upload one where this is specified" |

**Review rules that follow, and they are hard:**

1. **Never report a short application CV as a weakness on length alone.** A two-page CV from a
   prospective PhD student is normal, not thin. Northeastern states it plainly for applicants:
   "Don't worry about having short sections or a single page. A CV with two research
   experiences, a poster presentation, and relevant coursework is appropriate."
2. **Never recommend exhaustiveness to an applicant.** The comprehensiveness norm belongs to a
   cumulative job-market CV.
3. **Check whether the target even asks for a CV**, and for its stated length limit, before
   assessing length at all. Where that is unknown, say so rather than assuming a norm.
4. Where the user *is* on the academic job market, the job-market column applies instead — and
   whole-CV structure work belongs to `apply-cv-builder` either way.

Oxford's applicant page (https://www.ox.ac.uk/admissions/graduate/application-guide/supporting-documents/cv-resume)
returned 403 to direct fetch; its quotes above were recovered from search-engine snippets of that
URL, not from reading the page. Treat them as strong but unverified, and re-check the live page
before relying on the exact wording. Its guidance on personal data and unpublished work could not
be recovered at all — do not fill that gap from memory.

## Convention — four or more sources agree

- **A CV is not a résumé**: longer, more comprehensive, and not rewritten per position
  (MIT, Northeastern, Harvard GSAS, Yale, Berkeley ×2).
- **Ordering is strongest-first and field-dependent, not fixed**, once contact and education
  lead. Berkeley: "There is no standard format or recipe… the key organizing principle
  [is] what will make the most effective presentation." MIT: put what is "of primary
  importance" first. Harvard GSAS: "most important information should be on the first page."
  So **do not report a non-canonical section order as an error** — report it only where the
  order buries the applicant's strongest evidence.
- Reverse-chronological within sections (Harvard GSAS, Yale, Berkeley — weaker, 3 sources).

## Contested — route to the live call, do not assert

- **Length.** See the table above. This is the sharpest conflict of the nine.
- **Personal data.** Northeastern refuses to prescribe and flags country variance: expectations
  "(such as including a photo, date of birth, or nationality) vary significantly by country. Do
  not assume American CV conventions apply internationally." Berkeley gives a flat US-facing
  exclusion instead (below). Nothing reconciles them. Per the family-wide rule, do **not** tell a
  user that some country expects a photo or date of birth; include such fields only when the call
  or template asks.
- **Exhaustive vs. selective.** MIT and Northeastern lean exhaustive; Oxford and Harvard GSAS's
  archival-CV-then-tailor practice lean selective.

## Single-source — usable as a suggestion, never as a rule

- **Marital status, children, place of birth: omit** (Berkeley Part 2 only). Yale separately says
  omit hobbies and unrelated work.
- **Unpublished work** (Berkeley Part 2 only, but the most developed treatment of the nine, and
  directly relevant to this skill's credibility checks): "Forthcoming" only where genuinely
  accepted — not submitted or under review; use a separate "Work Submitted for Publication"
  heading with an explicit status ("under review", "accepted pending revisions"); prospective
  "Works in Progress" titles are acceptable. Berkeley also normalizes the absence of
  publications for new PhDs in the humanities and social sciences. Treat a "forthcoming" label on
  merely-submitted work as an inflated claim in the adversarial pass.
- **Bullet mechanics** (Harvard GSAS only): begin with an active verb, use sentence fragments,
  avoid personal pronouns, minimize articles. Style, not signal — Priority C at most.
- **Narrative CV** (Oxford Careers only, https://www.careers.ox.ac.uk/node/768281): shifts from
  "a list of your experience and achievements" to "descriptions of your contributions", and
  advises "highlight fewer key contributions in good detail, rather than provide long lists".
  Required by **UKRI, Wellcome, and other funders** via the R4RI framework (contributions to
  knowledge; development of others; the research environment; wider society) — this is a
  **funder and postdoc format**, not a PhD-admissions one. Apply it only where a specific
  studentship or fellowship call mandates it; verify against that call.

## Not covered by any source

No page gave quantification or metrics guidance for CV bullets, and none addressed inflation
risk beyond Berkeley's "forthcoming" caution. The bullet contract in `SKILL.md` is this skill's
own instrument, not a convention drawn from these sources — do not attribute it to them.
