# Address and formality conventions

How to open an email to an academic in the target system. This is the cheapest signal to get right and one of the most visible to get wrong — a misaddressed opening is read before a single word of your pitch.

**Determine the recipient's actual title from their institutional page**, not from their name, not from a directory listing, and never by inference. Faculty pages state how someone is titled and how they are addressed. When the page is ambiguous, the more formal option is the safe one.

---

## German-speaking systems (Germany, Austria, much of Switzerland)

Formal, title-bearing address is the default and omitting the title is a recognised mistake. University guidance for students is explicit and consistent on this.

**Form:** `Sehr geehrte Frau Professorin [Surname]` / `Sehr geehrter Herr Professor [Surname]`

Rules that matter:

- **Write "Professor" out** in the body of an email. "Prof." is for addresses and envelopes.
- **Professor absorbs the doctorate.** If someone is a professor you do not need to add *Dr.* as well. For someone who holds a doctorate but is not a professor, use `Doktor` / `Doktorin` (`Sehr geehrte Frau Doktor [Surname]`).
- **Never switch to first names unless explicitly invited.** The invitation is rare and comes from them.
- **Close formally:** `Mit freundlichen Grüßen`, then your full name.
- Writing in English to a German professor is normal and usually fine; the formality expectation carries over. `Dear Professor [Surname]` is the correct English rendering — not "Dear Prof. Dr. Surname," which reads as a mechanical transliteration.

The gendered title forms (`Professorin` / `Professor`, `Frau` / `Herr`) are grammatical in German. Take the form from how the person is titled on their own institutional page. Do not derive it from their name.

## Italian system

Formal address is expected. `Gentile Professore` / `Gentile Professoressa` is a safe formal opening; `Egregio Professore` is more formal still. `Chiarissimo/a Professore/ssa` (often abbreviated `Ch.mo`) is a traditional academic honorific that still appears in formal correspondence and official calls.

Writing in English is generally accepted at research institutions, but formality expectations remain. `Dear Professor [Surname]` works.

Italian institutional processes are frequently governed by a published **bando** (call). Where one exists, its stated contact route and required channel override informal outreach — check it before emailing.

## Anglophone systems (US, UK, Australia, Canada, Ireland)

`Dear Professor [Surname]` is correct and safe across all of them. `Dear Dr. [Surname]` is appropriate where the person is not a full professor; in the UK and Australia "Professor" is a rank rather than a general term for academic staff, so `Dr.` is often the accurate title and is not a demotion.

Register is markedly less formal than the German or Italian norm, and moving to first names after their reply is normal — follow whatever signature they use in their response.

## Nordic and Dutch systems

Notably informal by comparison. First names are common in academic correspondence and titles are used lightly. `Dear [First name]` is frequently acceptable, and heavy honorific stacking can read as stiff or distant rather than respectful.

When uncertain, open with `Dear Professor [Surname]` and follow their lead from the reply — this is the general fallback everywhere.

## Where you cannot determine the system

Use `Dear Professor [Surname]`, write in English, keep it short, and mirror their register in every subsequent message. No one is offended by correct-but-slightly-formal. The failure mode is over-familiarity, not over-politeness.

---

## What does not vary

The conventions above govern the opening and the register. They do **not** change what makes the email work: specificity about their actual research, brevity, a clear ask, and something that could not have been sent to anyone else. A perfectly addressed generic email is still a generic email.

## Volatility

Individual preference overrides national convention, and some academics say so on their own pages ("please just call me X"). Institutional style guides change. Treat everything here as the default when you have no better information, and treat the recipient's own stated preference, their page, and their reply signature as authoritative over it.
