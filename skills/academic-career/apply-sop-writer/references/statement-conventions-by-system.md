# Statement conventions across systems

**Epistemic note, read first.** The naming and document-requirement differences below are checkable facts. The claims about *register* — how personal, how narrative, how formal a statement should be — are **weakly sourced folklore**, widely repeated and inconsistently reported across sources. Treat them as hypotheses to check against the actual prompt, never as rules to assert to the user. When this file and the call's own prompt disagree, the prompt wins, always.

---

## The document is defined by the prompt, not by its name

"Statement of purpose," "letter of motivation," "personal statement," and "research statement" are used inconsistently and overlap heavily. The terminology splits roughly along regional lines — *statement of purpose* is more common in the US, *motivation letter* in Europe — but the same word can mean different documents at two universities in the same country.

**Always establish, from the call itself:** the exact prompt, the word or page limit, whether a separate research proposal is also required, and whether a personal-history or diversity statement is a distinct document. Do not infer these from the document's name. Where the user has not supplied the prompt, ask for it — this is the single highest-value piece of missing information and it changes the whole draft.

## Structural differences that are real

- **A named position or project changes the document.** Where the application is to a *specific funded project or advertised PhD position* (much of Europe, MSCA/EURAXESS, UK project studentships, Australian projects with a named supervisor), the statement must argue fit to **that project** and demonstrate the applicant has read its description. Where the application is to a **department or cohort** (typical US PhD admissions), the statement argues research direction and fit to several potential advisors, since the specific advisor is not yet fixed.
- **A separate research proposal may carry the research content.** Where one is required, the statement should not duplicate it. Where none is required, the statement absorbs more of that load.
- **Mission-driven scholarships add a scored dimension.** Programmes with a development, bilateral, or leadership mandate weight what the applicant will do afterwards, and often specifically their return to or contribution to their home country. This is a scored criterion in those competitions, not a closing sentiment. Where it applies, it needs concrete, plausible content.
- **Master's-level statements are not junior doctoral statements.** For a taught master's, programme and curriculum choice, the bridge from prior study, and post-degree plans carry the document. Do not push an applicant to invent a research proposal the call did not ask for.

## Register — hypotheses, not rules

Commonly reported, inconsistently evidenced, and to be checked rather than asserted:

- US statements are said to tolerate more personal narrative and origin story; European motivation letters are said to be more factual and more directly tied to the advertised position. Sources conflict on this, including on which direction is more "personal."
- Some European calls expect an actual **letter** — formal salutation, addressed to a named committee or professor, signed — rather than a titled essay. This one is easy to verify from the call and worth checking, since format mismatch is visible immediately.

**How to handle register in practice:** read the prompt's own verbs. A prompt asking *"why are you motivated to apply for this position"* is asking a different question from *"describe your research background and goals."* Answer the question actually asked, in the length actually allowed. That instruction is reliable; national-register generalisation is not.

## Self-presentation and credit

Statements are where applicants most often understate their own contribution — writing "we developed" and "I was involved in" for work they personally did. This is a **precision** problem before it is a cultural one: an admissions reader cannot award credit for a contribution the document does not attribute.

Calibrate toward accuracy in both directions: never inflate a claim, and never leave real work unattributed. Where an applicant is uncomfortable stating their own contribution directly, ask what specifically they did — the discomfort usually marks the exact sentence that needs fixing, and the fix is factual specificity rather than louder language.

Do not coach a persona and do not make claims about what people from any country are like.

## Language

Write in the language the call specifies. A statement in a second language should be read by a fluent speaker before submission — committees reliably distinguish awkward phrasing (forgivable) from an argument they cannot follow (not).

---

## Volatility

Prompts, word limits, required-document sets, and whether a research proposal is separate are **set per programme and per cycle**. Verify against the live call. Nothing in this file overrides the prompt in front of the user.
