# Evidence for structured answers and self-presentation calibration

Per this repo's epistemic-flagging convention, claims are tagged by what the evidence actually supports. Do not promote a claim to a stronger tier when talking to the user.

---

## STAR — provenance

**Established.** STAR was created by DDI (Development Dimensions International) in 1974 as part of their Targeted Selection programme. The components are Situation/Task (context), Action (what the individual did), Result (what was achieved and why it worked).

DDI also defines **STAR/AR**, appending Alternative Action and Alternative Result — used for developmental feedback, and directly useful for failure and weakness questions, where the alternative pair converts a setback into demonstrated judgement.

DDI's stated guidance to interviewers is worth knowing because it tells a candidate what is being extracted: ask open-ended "tell me about a time" questions, **focus on individual actions rather than team efforts**, and use follow-ups to complete the picture. The second point is the operative one for candidates — an answer that stays in "we" fails the instrument by design.

---

## Why structure helps — and what the evidence does *not* say

**Supported, with an important scope limit.** Schmidt & Hunter (1998, *Psychological Bulletin*), a meta-analysis of 85 years of personnel-selection research, reported operational validity of **r = .51 for structured interviews** versus **r = .38 for unstructured**, placing structured interviews among the strongest available predictors of job performance. Sackett et al. (2022) reanalysed the corrections applied in that literature and argued structured interviews may be the strongest single predictor, above cognitive-ability tests.

**The scope limit matters and must be stated honestly if the user asks.** This research measures **validity for the interviewer** — how well a structured protocol predicts later performance. It is *not* evidence that a candidate who answers in STAR format is more likely to be admitted. No claim of that kind should be made.

What it does license: structured protocols are widely used precisely because they work, and they are built to extract situation, individual action, and result. A candidate who supplies those elements is answering the instrument as designed; one who supplies narrative without them forces the assessor to score an incomplete record. That is a reasonable inference, not a measured effect — present it as such.

**Do not claim** that STAR "gets you admitted", that it raises scores by any amount, or that academic selection committees use structured protocols as rigorously as corporate hiring. Academic panels are frequently *semi*-structured at best.

## Self-presentation across cultures

**Supported for the core effect; do not over-extend.** Schmid Mast, M., Frauendorfer, D., & Popovic, L. (2011). Self-promoting and modest job applicants in different cultures. *Journal of Personnel Psychology*, 10, 70–77.

The finding: recruiters from cultures that value self-promotion (the study used Canadian recruiters) were more inclined to hire self-promoting over modest applicants than recruiters from cultures placing higher value on modesty (Swiss). The mismatch runs in both directions — the penalty is on the *gap between* the applicant's self-presentation norm and the assessor's expectation, not on modesty as such.

Related work in the same literature reports that applicants from modesty-valuing cultural clusters tend to understate accomplishments, often by attributing outcomes to favourable circumstances rather than personal merit, and that this is read by self-promotion-expecting assessors as weaker contribution.

**What this licenses:** telling a user that *how explicitly they claim credit* is read differently by different assessors, and that understating a real contribution can cost them with a committee that expects direct claims.

**What this does not license:**

- **No national character claims.** These are population-level tendencies in recruiter and applicant samples, not properties of individuals. Never tell a user what they are like because of where they are from. Ask them.
- **Hofstede-derived national-dimension scores are contested** and methodologically criticised. Keep them out. They are the fastest route from "useful calibration" to "stereotype catalogue."
- The research is drawn from **employment** interviews. Academic selection is a related but not identical setting; treat the transfer as plausible, not demonstrated.

---

## The operating rule

Calibrate **how explicitly the user claims work they actually did**. The target is accuracy: the assessor's estimate of the user's contribution should match reality.

- Never invent or inflate a claim. That is not calibration, and it collapses under follow-up.
- Never coach a personality. Performed confidence is detected, and it costs more than reserve does.
- Do ask what feels uncomfortable to say aloud — the discomfort usually locates the gap precisely.
- Do check for the first-person gap: an answer narrating team achievement with no individual action is the single most common correctable weakness, and it is exactly what STAR's Action step exists to surface.
