# Who is across the table

The organising principle of this skill. Academic selection interviews differ by **assessor**, not by country. A German committee and an Australian committee assess similarly; a German committee and a German supervisor do not. Region enters as a modifier on self-presentation (see `structured-answer-evidence.md`), not as the top-level cut.

Everything below about a *specific programme* is volatile. Verify against the live official call and the invitation email.

---

## Type A — The funder's academic committee

**Where it appears:** DAAD research grants (doctoral) and study scholarships (master's); some Erasmus Mundus Joint Master consortia; national research funders generally.

**Who is in the room:** typically 2–4 people — academics in or near the field, sometimes with a programme administrator. Independent selection committees, not the host university's admissions office.

**What they are testing:** whether the *plan* is sound and whether the applicant is a good bet for the funder's money. DAAD's published criteria weight roughly: academic record ~40%, quality and feasibility of the study or research plan ~30%, motivation and personal suitability ~20%, language ~10%. (Verify current weighting per programme and cycle.) Named sub-criteria include the choice of host institution and whether contact has already been established, integration of the project into the applicant's career path, and the significance of the stay for further development.

**What this implies for preparation:**

- **Feasibility is the exam.** Be able to defend a timeline, name what could go wrong, and say what the fallback is. A plan with no acknowledged risk reads as naive, not confident.
- **Justify the host.** Why *this* institution, *this* group, *this* supervisor — and evidence of prior contact if it exists. This is an explicit scored criterion, not small talk.
- **The motivation letter is interview material.** Committees discuss the submitted letter during the interview. Re-read it beforehand; any drift between the letter and spoken answers is visible.
- **Career integration.** Connect the proposed work backwards to prior training and forwards to what comes after. "Why now, why this step" is scored.
- **Language.** Where the programme names a language criterion, expect it to be probed, at least lightly.

**Master's-route applicants (DAAD Study Scholarships, MSc-level national scholarships, interviewing EMJM consortia):** the same committee structure, but the object under examination is a **study plan, not a research proposal**. DAAD's criterion is explicitly the quality of the "study *or* research plan" — do not prepare an MSc applicant to defend a research timeline with fallbacks they do not have. What shifts:

- **Programme choice replaces project feasibility.** Be able to say why *this* degree, why *this* curriculum, and what specific modules or specialisations drove the choice. "It is a good university" is the failure answer.
- **Career integration carries more weight, not less.** For master's scholarships attached to a development, bilateral, or leadership mission, what the applicant does *after* is a scored criterion. It needs to be concrete and plausible, and it needs to connect to the home country where the programme's mission says so.
- **The bridge backwards matters.** Why this is the right next step from the applicant's existing degree and work — gaps or changes of field must be explained, not skated over.
- **A research direction still helps** where the master's is research-oriented or is an intended route to a PhD, but it is evidence of seriousness rather than the thing being scored. Do not oversell a proposal at this level.

**Common failure:** treating it as a research seminar. The committee is not primarily evaluating whether the science is exciting; it is evaluating whether the applicant will complete the degree, at that host, in that time.

## Type B — The prospective supervisor

**Where it appears:** direct PhD applications initiated by cold email; Australian RTP and similar HDR routes where the supervisor relationship precedes the formal application; MSCA Doctoral Networks and other positions advertised via EURAXESS.

**Who is in the room:** one academic, occasionally with a group member. Often framed as an informal chat. It is not informal.

**What they are testing:** whether they want to work with this person for three to five years. Independence of thought, whether the applicant has actually read their work, whether the applicant can hold a research conversation and disagree productively, and whether they will finish.

**What this implies for preparation:**

- **Read their recent work properly.** Not titles — at least one paper closely enough to ask a real question about a limitation or a next step. This is the strongest available signal and its absence is immediately obvious.
- **Prepare to think aloud, not to recite.** Supervisors frequently probe with open or half-formed questions to see how the applicant reasons. A polished monologue is worse than visible reasoning. Saying "I don't know, but here's how I'd find out" is a strong answer.
- **Have a direction, hold it loosely.** Arriving with no research interest reads as passive; arriving unwilling to adapt reads as unsupervisable.
- **The relationship is bidirectional.** Ask about supervision style, group size, meeting frequency, funding duration, what happens if the project shifts. In employment-contract systems (much of Northern Europe, MSCA), ask about contract length, salary, and terms — these are normal questions there, not impolite ones.

**Structural note:** in RTP-style routes, an informal supervisor agreement may be a precondition for the application to be considered at all — but this is set per university, not nationally. Confirm with the specific institution's graduate research office.

**Common failure:** treating it as an admissions interview and performing. Supervisors are trying to simulate what a weekly meeting with this person would feel like.

## Type C — The administrative or embassy panel

**Where it appears:** MAECI and comparable national government scholarships, frequently routed through embassies, consulates, or a national agency (Uni-Italia in the Italian case).

**Who is in the room:** programme administrators, embassy staff, sometimes an academic. Often not specialists in the applicant's field.

**What they are testing:** eligibility, motivation, coherence of the plan at a non-specialist level, and whether the applicant will do what the programme was funded to produce — frequently including return to and benefit for the home country.

**What this implies for preparation:**

- **Explain the research to a non-specialist.** The two-minute summary needs a version with no field jargon at all. Depth is not the exam here; clarity and purpose are.
- **Know your own eligibility cold.** Degree equivalence, age limits where they exist, language certification, financial-need criteria where applicable. Panels ask, and hesitation reads as an unresolved problem.
- **Be concrete about after.** Programmes with a development or bilateral mission weight what the applicant will do with the training. Vague answers here cost more than in type A or B.
- **Administrative accuracy matters.** Dates, institution names, programme titles. This panel is partly verifying the file.

**Common failure:** over-technical answers. The panel cannot evaluate the science and will score what it can evaluate — clarity, motivation, and plausibility.

## Type D — No interview

**Where it appears:** SISGP (Swedish Institute Scholarships for Global Professionals) selects on documents; Italian DSU regional grants are income-assessed (ISEE) administrative awards; IYT / "Invest Your Talent in Italy" and many Erasmus Mundus Joint Master consortia select on scored application files. Consortium practice varies — some EMJM programmes do interview, most score documents.

**What to do:** say plainly that there is no interview stage, and route out. The motivation letter, CV, and references carry the entire load — that is `apply-sop-writer`, `apply-cv-builder`, and `apply-recommendation-letter-strategist`, not this skill.

Two caveats worth raising with the user:

- Some document-only programmes use **mandatory templates**, where a non-conforming CV or reference letter is rejected without review. Check the call.
- A few use a **recorded video** in place of an interview. Prepare it as type A or C material with no follow-up questions: delivery and structure carry more weight, improvisation carries none.

**Degree-level and eligibility traps in this group** (all volatile — verify against the current call):

- SISGP and IYT fund **master's study only**, and SISGP additionally requires substantial documented work experience and leadership evidence.
- **Erasmus Mundus has no doctoral track.** Joint Doctorates ran their final intake in 2013 and were not renewed under Erasmus+. EU doctoral funding now runs through MSCA Doctoral Networks, advertised per-position on EURAXESS as salaried employment — which makes it assessor type B, not a scholarship application.
- Italian **DSU** eligibility for doctoral candidates is set regionally and is often restricted or excluded where a state doctoral stipend counts as incompatible income. Do not assume PhD eligibility; check the specific regional agency's call.
- **MAECI** has funded doctoral study but applies an age cap. Confirm the current limit and the degree levels in the live call.

## Using this file

1. Triage to a type before preparing anything.
2. If type D, route out — do not manufacture interview prep.
3. If the type is ambiguous, tell the user what to check rather than guessing.
4. On a mixed panel, prepare the dominant type and hold the other in reserve.
